2  Behind the Simulations

Written for this book by Giorgio Arcara. This chapter is not in Irizarry’s Introduction to Data Science.

Chapter 1 used a simulated five-item satisfaction scale. This chapter explains how it is generated. You can skip it and lose nothing from the argument of that chaptet, but if you want to understand why the simulations behave the way they do, or you want to build your own, this is where the machinery is explained.

2.1 Why simulate at all

There is one specific reason, and it is not just convenience.

Real data never hands you the actual “ground truth” of what’s happening or, in the case of psychological measurement, the true score. That is the entire problem Chapter 1 is about: you observe \(X\), you want to know about \(T\), and \(T\) is permanently out of reach. Every reliability coefficient, every validity argument, every correction for attenuation is an attempt to reason about a quantity nobody can see.

In a simulation, you start from a different perspective: you generate \(T\) yourself, and you see how well the methods are able to capture a known ground truth. That makes it the only setting in which you can check whether those techniques work, where you can compute a disattenuated correlation and then look up the answer. It is the difference between being told that Cronbach’s \(\alpha\) estimates reliability and watching it do so.

The cost is that simulated data is only as realistic as the model that produced it. So it is worth being able to read that model, and being clear about which model it is. Very often, you simulate scores with the same, or similar, generative model you use to test the hytpothesis, and this creates a sort of circularity: the method may work because you created data in the way the method expects.

2.2 What the Likert simulator models

Recall Stevens’ definition of measurement from Chapter 1: “the assignment of numerals to objects or events according to rules”. A Likert scale is a very literal case. A respondent has some position on a construct, and the questionnaire turns that position into one of the numerals 1 to 5. The interesting part is the rule that does the turning, because the rule decides what the numerals can be used for (Table 1.1).

simulate_likert() is a model of that rule. It has a latent construct, some noise, and a set of cut points that convert a continuous impression into an ordered category. It is designed to show what happens to your numbers when the rule is not the interval-scale ideal — which is the question about Likert data that Chapter 1 cares most about.

It borrows the language of Classical Test Theory (CTT) for one of its steps, but it is not a CTT simulation. We come back to this after building it, in Section 2.4.

2.3 The Likert simulator

simulate_likert() generates responses to a multi-item scale in four steps. We will build each one by hand, then check the result against the package function.

Step 1: the latent construct

Every respondent gets a position on the latent construct, on a standardised scale. The function calls it the true_score; we write it \(T\).

set.seed(1)
n <- 400
true_score <- rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
head(round(true_score, 2))
#> [1] -0.63  0.18 -0.84  1.60  0.33 -0.82

The units are arbitrary — a standard deviation of satisfaction. Nothing observable is in these numbers yet.

When you ask for groups, the group shifts this distribution. group_effect moves the mean; group_sd changes the spread:

group <- rep(c("standard", "redesigned"), length.out = n)
shift <- ifelse(group == "standard", 0, 0.5)   # group_effect = 0.5
true_grouped <- rnorm(n, mean = shift, sd = 1)
tapply(true_grouped, group, mean)
#> redesigned   standard 
#>     0.4489    -0.0904

Keeping those two knobs separate matters. Two groups can share a mean and differ in spread — some populations really are more homogeneous than others — and Chapter 1 uses exactly that case to show that a difference in scores is not always a difference in the construct.

Step 2: noise in each item’s latent response

Each item is an imperfect window onto the same construct. Every item gets its own independent error:

n_items <- 5
error_sd <- 1

latent <- matrix(true_score, nrow = n, ncol = n_items) +
  matrix(rnorm(n * n_items, mean = 0, sd = error_sd),
         nrow = n, ncol = n_items)

round(latent[1:3, ], 2)
#>       [,1]  [,2]  [,3]  [,4]  [,5]
#> [1,] -1.71 -2.18 -0.28 -1.51 -1.00
#> [2,] -1.64  2.11  0.20 -1.74  1.18
#> [3,]  0.16 -2.69 -1.71  0.78 -0.73

Each row is one respondent; each column is one item. The construct is constant along a row, and what varies is the error.

This looks like CTT’s \(X = T + E\), and at this level it is: each value is \(T\) plus independent, zero-mean error. But notice what these numbers are. They are each respondent’s latent response to an item — the continuous impression in their head — and nobody ever records them. The observed score comes later, after step 3, and by then \(X = T + E\) no longer holds exactly.

error_sd is the main dial on reliability: more error, less reliable scale. But it is not reliability itself, and the function never takes a reliability value as input. Reliability is something you estimate on what the function returns, as Chapter 1 does.

Step 3: the ordinal response process

Here is the step that matters most, and the one that is invisible in real data. This is Stevens’ rule.

A respondent does not report a latent quantity. They pick a category. Somewhere in their head, a continuous impression gets converted into one of five boxes, and the conversion happens at thresholds — the points at which they switch from choosing “3” to choosing “4”.

thresholds <- qnorm(1:4 / 5) * 1.3 - 0.85
round(thresholds, 2)
#> [1] -1.94 -1.18 -0.52  0.24

Those are the package defaults. Notice they are not symmetric: they sit well below zero, which means most of the latent distribution falls into the highest categories. That is deliberate, and it is what satisfaction and usability scales actually look like — ratings pile up at the top and the scale runs out of room. This is a ceiling.

Cutting the latent values at those thresholds gives the responses:

items <- matrix(findInterval(latent, thresholds) + 1L,
                nrow = n, ncol = n_items)
table(items[, 1])
#> 
#>   1   2   3   4   5 
#>  32  45  68  88 167

Compare that with evenly spaced thresholds — the idealised response process in which the item behaves like an interval-scale measure:

thresholds_even <- qnorm(1:4 / 5)
round(thresholds_even, 2)
#> [1] -0.84 -0.25  0.25  0.84

items_even <- matrix(findInterval(latent, thresholds_even) + 1L,
                     nrow = n, ncol = n_items)
table(items_even[, 1])
#> 
#>   1   2   3   4   5 
#> 111  61  62  46 120

Identical people. Identical latent positions. Identical noise. Completely different-looking data, because the rule that assigns the numerals differs. Figure 2.1 shows what the two sets of cut points do to the same latent distribution.

In both panels the numerals are correctly ordered: a higher latent value never produces a lower response. That is all an ordinal scale promises. Only the evenly spaced rule also makes the distances between numerals roughly comparable, and even that is an idealisation.

Step 4: the total

Finally the items are summed, which is what almost every scale does:

total <- rowSums(items)
head(total)
#> [1] 12 18 15 24 23 18

Summing is itself an assumption worth noticing. It treats all five items as equally important and as equally spaced, which is the interval assumption arriving through the back door: you are adding numerals that, by Stevens’ classification, only license ordering.

Checking against the package

The four steps above are exactly what the function does. To prove it, let us run them again in one block, in the order the function uses them, so that the random draws line up, and compare:

set.seed(1)
t_hand <- rnorm(400)
lat_hand <- matrix(t_hand, 400, 5) + matrix(rnorm(400 * 5), 400, 5)
total_hand <- rowSums(matrix(findInterval(lat_hand, thresholds) + 1L, 400, 5))

from_package <- simulate_likert(n = 400, n_items = 5, error_sd = 1, seed = 1)

all.equal(total_hand, from_package$total)
#> [1] TRUE
all.equal(t_hand, from_package$true_score)
#> [1] TRUE

Identical, down to the last respondent. (The rebuild is necessary because the group demonstration in step 1 drew its own random numbers, which shifts everything drawn afterwards — a small reminder that in simulation work the order of your random draws is part of your results.)

If you want to read the real thing, it is one command away:

behdslabs:::.simulate_likert

I would encourage you to do that, in the spirit of the point made in Part 1 about AI-assisted code: you are responsible for analyses built on code you have not read.

Retesting the same people

true_score is both returned and accepted as an argument. That is what makes a retest expressible: the same people, the same construct, a new occasion with fresh noise.

t1 <- simulate_likert(n = 300, seed = 21)
t2 <- simulate_likert(n = 300, true_score = t1$true_score, seed = 22)

identical(t1$true_score, t2$true_score)   # same people, same construct
#> [1] TRUE
cor(t1$total, t2$total)                   # but not the same scores
#> [1] 0.817

2.4 Is this Classical Test Theory?

Not quite, and it is worth being precise about why, because the simulator uses the same letters CTT uses.

In CTT, \(X = T + E\) is a statement about the observed score — here, the total. \(T\) is defined as the average total a person would get over infinitely many administrations, and \(E\) is whatever is left over. Reliability is the share of the variance of \(X\) that is due to \(T\):

\[\text{reliability} = \frac{\sigma^2_T}{\sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E}\]

simulate_likert() puts \(T + E\) in a different place: on each item’s latent response, before the response rule is applied. Three things follow.

  1. The observed total is not the latent \(T\) plus random error. Between the two sits the response rule, which cuts, bounds and sums. The CTT true score of the total — the average total over many retests — is a curved function of the latent \(T\), not a copy of it. Figure 2.2 shows the curve: it flattens at the top, where the scale runs out of room.
  2. The error in the total depends on \(T\). For a respondent near the ceiling, the error can pull the total down but hardly push it up. This is exactly the CTT limitation flagged in Chapter 1: on a bounded scale, \(T\) and \(E\) stop being independent.
  3. Reliability is never set. It emerges from error_sd, the thresholds and the number of items. You find it by analysing the output, as with a real instrument.

We can measure the gap. If we could record the latent responses and sum them, CTT would hold exactly, and the reliability of that latent total would be \(k / (k + \sigma^2_E)\) for \(k\) items and a latent \(T\) with SD 1. Compare it with what you actually get from the recorded responses: Cronbach’s \(\alpha\), and the squared correlation between the total and the latent \(T\), which under pure CTT would both equal the reliability.

cronbach_alpha <- function(items) {
  k <- ncol(items)
  k / (k - 1) * (1 - sum(apply(items, 2, var)) / var(rowSums(items)))
}

for (e in c(0.5, 1, 2, 3)) {
  d <- simulate_likert(n = 5000, error_sd = e, seed = 3)
  its <- as.matrix(d[, paste0("item_", 1:5)])
  cat(sprintf("error_sd = %.1f   latent CTT rel = %.2f   alpha = %.2f   cor(total, true)^2 = %.2f\n",
              e, 5 / (5 + e^2), cronbach_alpha(its),
              cor(d$total, d$true_score)^2))
}
#> error_sd = 0.5   latent CTT rel = 0.95   alpha = 0.93   cor(total, true)^2 = 0.85
#> error_sd = 1.0   latent CTT rel = 0.83   alpha = 0.80   cor(total, true)^2 = 0.75
#> error_sd = 2.0   latent CTT rel = 0.56   alpha = 0.49   cor(total, true)^2 = 0.48
#> error_sd = 3.0   latent CTT rel = 0.36   alpha = 0.30   cor(total, true)^2 = 0.29

The three columns move together, and the bigger error_sd is, the lower they all are. That is why the simulator works well for the demonstrations in Chapter 1: retest correlations, \(\alpha\) and the correction for attenuation all behave approximately as CTT says they should. But the columns are not equal. Turning a latent impression into a category loses information of its own, so the recorded scale is somewhat less reliable than the latent one, and the total tracks the latent \(T\) less well than \(\alpha\) would suggest.

None of this is a defect. It is what the simulator is for: it shows what the response rule does to your numbers. It is just not the tool for illustrating CTT in its pure form.

2.5 Simulating CTT directly

If what you want is CTT itself — an observed score that is exactly a true score plus independent error, with a reliability you choose — the simulation is simpler, and it starts from the reliability rather than from the error.

Rearrange the definition of reliability to find how much error you need:

\[\text{rel} = \frac{\sigma^2_T}{\sigma^2_T + \sigma^2_E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_E = \sigma_T \sqrt{\frac{1 - \text{rel}}{\text{rel}}}\]

Then draw true scores, draw errors with that SD, and add them:

simulate_ctt <- function(n, rel, mu = 0, sd = 1, true_score = NULL) {
  if (rel <= 0 || rel >= 1) stop("rel must be between 0 and 1.")
  sd_error <- sd * sqrt((1 - rel) / rel)
  if (is.null(true_score)) true_score <- rnorm(n, mean = mu, sd = sd)
  observed <- true_score + rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = sd_error)
  data.frame(true_score = true_score, observed = observed)
}

set.seed(5)
ctt <- simulate_ctt(n = 5000, rel = 0.7, mu = 50, sd = 10)
head(round(ctt, 1))
#>   true_score observed
#> 1       41.6     34.5
#> 2       63.8     64.1
#> 3       37.4     29.8
#> 4       50.7     44.9
#> 5       67.1     59.8
#> 6       44.0     49.2

Here the reliability is an input, so you can check that it comes back out. Under CTT, the squared correlation between observed and true scores equals the reliability, and so does the correlation between two administrations to the same people:

for (r in c(0.9, 0.7, 0.5, 0.3)) {
  x1 <- simulate_ctt(n = 5000, rel = r, mu = 50, sd = 10)
  x2 <- simulate_ctt(n = 5000, rel = r, sd = 10, true_score = x1$true_score)
  cat(sprintf("rel = %.1f   cor(observed, true)^2 = %.2f   retest r = %.2f\n",
              r, cor(x1$observed, x1$true_score)^2,
              cor(x1$observed, x2$observed)))
}
#> rel = 0.9   cor(observed, true)^2 = 0.90   retest r = 0.90
#> rel = 0.7   cor(observed, true)^2 = 0.69   retest r = 0.70
#> rel = 0.5   cor(observed, true)^2 = 0.51   retest r = 0.49
#> rel = 0.3   cor(observed, true)^2 = 0.30   retest r = 0.31

Both columns land on the reliability you asked for, give or take sampling error. That is CTT working exactly — because we wrote its assumptions into the code.

That is also its limit. The observed scores are continuous and unbounded: nothing stops one from being 112 on a test whose maximum is 100. There are no response categories, no thresholds and no ceiling, and the error is independent of \(T\) by construction. Stevens’ question — what the numerals can be used for — does not even arise. This simulator cannot show you the problems of Likert data, because it has no Likert data in it.

2.6 Two simulators, two questions

The two simulators answer different questions, and it helps to pick the one that matches yours.

Chapter 1 uses simulate_likert() throughout, also for reliability and attenuation, because one running example is easier to follow than two. The CTT results it shows there hold approximately, for the reasons given in Section 2.4.

2.7 Building your own

Neither function is complicated, and the point of this chapter is that you could have written both. If you want to explore a measurement question that they do not cover, the pattern is the same one Chapter 1 describes in words:

  1. Decide which question the simulation is for, and therefore which model you need.
  2. Generate the construct.
  3. Add whatever contaminates the observation of it.
  4. Apply whatever process turns that observation into a recorded value.
  5. Analyse the result as if you did not know steps 2 to 4 — then check your answer against them.

Step 5 is the one people skip, and it is the only one that tells you whether your analysis works.
