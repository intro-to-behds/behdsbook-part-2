24  From Results to Conclusions

Running a statistical test is the easy part of an analysis. R computes a p-value, a confidence interval and an effect size in a fraction of a second. What R cannot do is say what those numbers mean: whether the effect is causal, whom it applies to, and how much we should trust it. That step is interpretation, and it is the analyst’s job.

Interpretation does not mean that any reading of the results is as good as another. A good interpretation brings together all the available evidence: how the data were collected (Chapter 4), how well the variables were measured (Chapter 1), what the estimates and their uncertainty say (Chapter 13, Chapter 21), what is missing (Chapter 23), and how the analysis was done (Chapter 3). Some interpretations are better than others because they account for more of this evidence.

This chapter is about the step from results to conclusions. The first half is about causal claims: when an association can be read as a cause, and what else can produce it. The second half is about interpreting a result in general, and ends with a list of common mistakes.

24.1 Explanation and prediction

Data analysis can answer two different kinds of question.

  • Prediction: which users are likely to stop using an app next month? A model that predicts well is useful even if we do not know why it works. The number of days since the last login may predict dropout very well, without being its cause.
  • Explanation: why do users stop using the app? Here we want to know the causes.

The difference matters because we usually want causes for a practical reason: a cause tells us what to change. Knowing that users with many notifications report more stress may help us predict who is stressed. It does not tell us whether reducing notifications would reduce stress. Only a causal relationship tells us what would happen if we intervened. Most questions in behavioural research and in the design of technologies are, in the end, questions about interventions: should we change the interface, send fewer notifications, offer the training? These need causal answers.

As discussed in Chapter 4, a causal claim is about a comparison we can never fully observe: what would have happened to the same person, at the same time, without the intervention. Randomised experiments solve this problem on average, across groups. Without randomisation, we must argue that the groups we compare are comparable, and this argument can fail in several ways.

24.2 Correlational designs and correlation analyses

The phrase correlation does not imply causation is often misread as a statement about a statistical technique. It is a statement about the design. A correlation coefficient computed on data from a randomised experiment can support a causal conclusion; a t-test computed on data from an observational study cannot, on its own.

Consider a study comparing patients with a neurological condition and healthy controls on a reaction-time task, or heavy and light smartphone users on an attention test. The analysis is a comparison of two groups, with a t-test or a Wilcoxon test (Chapter 17). It looks like an experiment, but nobody assigned participants to the groups: they arrived already belonging to one of them. Such pre-existing groups differ in many ways besides the one that names them. Patients and controls may differ in age, education, medication, fatigue and motivation; heavy smartphone users may differ in age, sleep and job. The study is correlational in design, whatever test is used to analyse it.

Matching participants on some characteristics, such as age and education, makes the groups more comparable on those characteristics, but not on the ones that were not measured. This is why randomisation, which also balances unmeasured characteristics on average, is so valuable.

24.3 Alternative explanations

When two variables are associated, the causal reading (X causes Y) is only one of several explanations. A good interpretation lists the others and asks whether the design rules them out. The main ones are chance, reverse causation, confounding, and selection.

Chance

Any association can arise by chance in a sample. The p-value (Chapter 14) quantifies how surprising the data would be if there were no association, and the risk grows when many associations are tested and only the significant ones are reported (Chapter 19). Chance is the alternative that statistics handles best; the others are not addressed by any p-value.

Reverse causation

Sometimes the effect causes the putative cause. People who feel stressed are the ones most likely to install a stress-reduction app. The following data are simulated so that we know the truth: the probability of using the app increases with a person’s baseline stress, and using the app reduces stress by 0.5 points on a 10-point scale.

set.seed(1983)
n <- 500
baseline <- rnorm(n, 5, 1.5)
app <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-6 + 1.1 * baseline))
stress <- baseline - 0.5 * app + rnorm(n, 0, 0.5)
stress_app <- tibble(app = factor(app, labels = c("non-user", "user")),
                     stress)

stress_app |>
  group_by(app) |>
  summarise(mean_stress = mean(stress), n = n())
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   app      mean_stress     n
#>   <fct>          <dbl> <int>
#> 1 non-user        4.44   289
#> 2 user            5.46   211
t.test(stress ~ app, data = stress_app)$p.value
#> [1] 4.19e-15

App users are clearly more stressed than non-users, and the difference is highly significant. Reading it causally would be backwards: it would say that the app increases stress, when we built the data so that it lowers it. Nothing is wrong with the test or the code; the interpretation is wrong. Only a design that compares the same people before and after, with a control group, or that assigns the app at random, could estimate its effect.

Confounding

A confounder is a variable that influences both the putative cause and the outcome, creating an association between them. Chapter 4 shows an example: workload increases both the number of notifications and stress.

Confounding can even reverse an association. The app_task_completion dataset records, for six in-app tasks (A to F), how many attempts users made on mobile and on desktop, and the percentage of attempts that were completed. Overall, mobile attempts are completed more often:

app_task_completion |>
  group_by(device) |>
  summarise(completion = sum(completion_rate * n_attempts) / sum(n_attempts),
            attempts = sum(n_attempts))
#> # A tibble: 2 × 3
#>   device  completion attempts
#>   <chr>        <dbl>    <dbl>
#> 1 desktop       30.3     3670
#> 2 mobile        44.5     5382

Mobile looks about 14 points better. Now look at each task separately, a technique called stratifying:

app_task_completion |>
  ggplot(aes(task, completion_rate, colour = device, size = n_attempts)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.8) +
  labs(x = "Task", y = "Completion rate (%)", size = "Attempts") +
  theme_minimal()

Task by task, the two devices are similar, and for task A desktop is better. The overall difference comes from the tasks: tasks A and B are easy, and most of their attempts were made on mobile, while desktop users spent most of their attempts on the hard tasks C to F. Task difficulty influences both the device used and the chance of completing the task. When the overall comparison and the stratified ones point in different directions, the result is called Simpson’s paradox.

This dataset has the same structure as a classic real example: graduate admissions by sex at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1973 (P. J. Bickel, E. A. Hammel & J. W. O’Connell, Science, 1975).

Stratifying works only for confounders that we thought of and measured. In an observational study, there may always be others.

Selection

The people in a dataset are rarely a random sample of the population we care about. Only some people agree to take part in a study, only some users leave a review, and only some participants stay until the end. If the process that brings people into the data is related to the variables we study, it can create, hide or distort associations. This is selection bias.

A subtle form of selection occurs when the selection depends on two variables at once. Suppose an app store features an app if it has either an excellent design or excellent marketing. In the simulated data below, design quality and marketing quality are unrelated across all apps. Among the featured apps, they are negatively correlated: a featured app with poor marketing must have an excellent design, and vice versa.

set.seed(2026)
apps <- tibble(design = rnorm(1000), marketing = rnorm(1000)) |>
  mutate(featured = design + marketing > 1.5)

cor(apps$design, apps$marketing)
#> [1] -0.0174
apps |> filter(featured) |> summarise(r = cor(design, marketing), n = n())
#> # A tibble: 1 × 2
#>        r     n
#>    <dbl> <int>
#> 1 -0.673   123

Looking only at the featured apps, we could conclude that good marketing comes at the cost of design. The association was created by the selection. A variable that is influenced by two others, like featured here, is called a collider, and selecting or adjusting on it creates spurious associations. The same mechanism operates when a study includes only participants who completed it, or only patients who were referred to a clinic.

Drawing the alternatives

Confounders, colliders and a third structure, the mediator (a variable through which the cause acts, such as app use → fewer notifications → less stress), can be drawn as diagrams in which arrows point from causes to effects:

Such diagrams are called directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). They are a formal tool for deciding which variables must be taken into account to estimate a causal effect, and which must not. They are beyond the scope of this book; Rohrer (2018), in the further reading, is an accessible introduction.

Adjusting is not free

A common reaction to confounding is to include in the analysis every variable that might matter. This is not always safe. Adjusting for a confounder removes a spurious association, but adjusting for a mediator removes part of the effect we want to estimate, and adjusting for a collider creates an association that was not there, as in the app-store example. Which variables to include in a model is therefore a causal decision, not only a statistical one. The question returns when we fit models with several predictors, in the chapters on regression.

24.4 Towards causal claims from observational data

When an experiment is impossible, because it would be unethical, too expensive, or because the causes cannot be manipulated, causal questions must be studied with observational data. No single observational study settles a causal question, but some features make a causal reading more plausible. A classic list comes from the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill (1965), who discussed smoking and lung cancer. Among his criteria:

  • Temporality: the cause comes before the effect. Longitudinal designs can show this; cross-sectional designs usually cannot.
  • Strength and dose–response: a strong association, which increases with the amount of exposure.
  • Consistency: the association is found by different studies, in different samples, with different methods.
  • Plausibility: there is a credible mechanism linking cause and effect.
  • Experiment: when the exposure is changed, the outcome changes.

None of these is a proof. They are a way of organising the evidence, and they are useful questions to ask of any causal claim based on observational data.

24.5 Interpreting a result

Three questions

Whatever the design, three questions help to interpret a result:

  1. What was the design? Was anything manipulated? Were participants randomly assigned? Who took part, and who was left out?
  2. What do the results actually support? How large is the effect, how precise is the estimate, and which conclusions are compatible with it?
  3. What are the alternative explanations? Chance, reverse causation, confounding, selection, measurement problems.

Read the numbers together

A p-value alone says little. The same p-value can come from a large effect estimated imprecisely in a small study, or from a tiny effect estimated precisely in a very large one. In the simulated example below, both studies give a p-value between 0.01 and 0.02:

set.seed(30)
small_study <- tibble(group = rep(c("A", "B"), each = 15),
                      score = c(rnorm(15, 0, 1), rnorm(15, 1.05, 1)))
large_study <- tibble(group = rep(c("A", "B"), each = 2000),
                      score = c(rnorm(2000, 0, 1), rnorm(2000, 0.08, 1)))

summarise_test <- function(d) {
  tt <- t.test(score ~ group, data = d)
  tibble(n_per_group = nrow(d) / 2,
         difference = unname(diff(tt$estimate)),
         ci_low = -tt$conf.int[2], ci_high = -tt$conf.int[1],
         p = tt$p.value)
}
bind_rows(small = summarise_test(small_study),
          large = summarise_test(large_study), .id = "study")
#> # A tibble: 2 × 6
#>   study n_per_group difference ci_low ci_high      p
#>   <chr>       <dbl>      <dbl>  <dbl>   <dbl>  <dbl>
#> 1 small          15     0.963  0.219    1.71  0.0131
#> 2 large        2000     0.0770 0.0146   0.139 0.0155

The small study suggests a large difference, about one standard deviation, but its confidence interval is wide: the data are compatible with effects from small to very large. The large study estimates a difference of less than a tenth of a standard deviation, precisely: the effect is almost certainly real, and almost certainly small. Reporting the estimate, its confidence interval and an effect size (Chapter 13, Chapter 21) next to the p-value makes the difference visible.

Non-significant is not “no effect”

A p-value above 0.05 does not show that there is no effect (Chapter 14). A study with low power (Chapter 22) can easily miss a real effect. Look at the confidence interval: if it includes both zero and effects that would matter, the study is inconclusive, not negative.

Missing data and analytic choices

Two further questions concern how the final numbers were obtained. Who is in the analysis? If participants dropped out or skipped questions for reasons related to the outcome, the remaining sample may be optimistic (Chapter 23). How many choices were made, and when? Exclusion rules, outcome measures and covariates can be chosen in many reasonable ways. When they are chosen after seeing the data, a significant result is much easier to obtain, and much less informative (Chapter 3).

Why this matters: the replication crisis

Many published findings in psychology could not be replicated by later studies (see Section 3.9.1). The causes were not mainly fraud, but ordinary practices: small studies with low power, flexible analyses, and the preference of journals for significant results. Each of these leads to results that are interpreted with more confidence than the evidence supports. Careful interpretation, which reads the design, the estimates and the analytic choices together, is the first defence.

24.6 Common interpretation mistakes

The following mistakes are frequent in papers, reports and the press. Most of them follow from forgetting one of the three questions above.

Hidden causal claims. Causal claims are often made without the word cause. Verbs such as leads to, boosts, elicits, protects or improves all describe causal effects. With observational data, the defensible wording is at the top of the following ladder:

Forgetting confounders. “Users of the meditation app sleep better” invites the question: who installs a meditation app? Probably people who already care about their health.

Forgetting who took part. Only some people consent to a study, only motivated users answer a satisfaction survey, and most participants in psychology studies come from a narrow range of countries and backgrounds (Henrich et al., 2010). A result holds for the people who could have been in the data.

“Significant in one group, not in the other”. Suppose an app reduces stress significantly in women (p = 0.03) but not in men (p = 0.20). It is tempting to conclude that the app works for women and not for men. But the two estimates can be very similar; the difference between them must be tested directly, as an interaction (see Chapter 18). The difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not itself statistically significant (Gelman & Stern, 2006).

Forgetting the strength of the evidence. One small study with p = 0.04 is weak evidence, however interesting the result. Effect size, precision, power and replication all matter.

Averages are not individuals. A difference between group means does not mean that every individual differs in the same way. And an association between people (people who use their phone more are more stressed) need not hold within people (on days when I use my phone more, I am more stressed), as discussed in Chapter 4.

Regression to the mean. Participants selected because of extreme scores, such as the most stressed users, will on average score less extremely the next time, with or without an intervention. A single-group pre–post study cannot separate this from a real effect.

“We controlled for it”. Adjusting for a confounder removes its influence only as far as the confounder is well measured. A covariate measured with error leaves part of the confounding in place (see Measurement error and its consequences in Chapter 1), and confounders that were not measured are not controlled at all.

Going beyond the data. Results apply to the people, tasks, settings and range of values that were studied. A laboratory effect in students may not hold for older adults using their own phones at home.

Exploratory results presented as confirmatory. A pattern found while exploring the data is a hypothesis for the next study, not a confirmed finding (see Two ways of exploring in Chapter 3).

24.8 Exercises

1. For each of the following studies, say whether the design is experimental, quasi-experimental or observational, and name one alternative explanation for the result. (a) Participants are randomly assigned to complete an attention task with or without notifications; those with notifications are slower. (b) Heavy smartphone users are slower than light users on the same task. (c) After a university introduced a notification-free policy during lectures, students’ exam grades rose compared with the previous year.

2. Using app_task_completion, compute the overall completion rate by device, and then the completion rate by device for tasks C to F only. Explain the difference in your own words.

3. Run the app-store simulation again with a threshold of 0 instead of 1.5, and then of 2.5. How does the correlation among featured apps change? Why?

4. Find a news article or a press release that reports a study on technology use and wellbeing. Which wording does it use (Table 24.2)? Is the wording supported by the design? Rewrite the headline so that it matches the design.

5. Run the same-p example again with 50 participants per group in the small study. How do the confidence interval and the p-value change? Which of the two studies would you rather cite to claim that the effect is important, and why?
